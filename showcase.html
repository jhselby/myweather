What the bar is

A person looking up the weather reads the National Weather Service forecast on weather.gov. That is the bar we beat, field by field, not a raw model that nobody reads.

Early on we compared ourselves to a raw model that runs about 3°F cold at night at the Cove. Beating that overstated the gain, so on November 15 we moved the bar to weather.gov and the numbers got smaller and more honest.

How we know it's real

The scoring is built so we can't flatter ourselves.

Truth we don't control

Temperature is scored against URMA, NOAA's hourly analysis built from satellites and surface reports. It doesn't use our stations or our corrections. Where only our stations are available we say so on the row.

Days it never saw

A change ships only if it wins on days it was never tuned on. Models and blend weights refit every morning from the last 14 days and are judged on what happens next.

The bar can move against us

When a number disagrees with reality we suspect the number first. The bar itself has been moved twice to a harder one, and the scoreboard shows the smaller gain.

Ideas we tested and killed

  • A European-model solar blend looked 25.7% better. It was a one-hour timing error in our own data; on aligned data it was zero.
  • A "bust-aware" temperature blend moved every field by less than half a percent. Rejected.
  • Calling the model's own output "observed": the scored observation was partly the model. We now score on stations alone and on URMA, and say which is which.
The neighborhood network

Dozens of stations within two and a half miles, each one corrected for its own habits.

Every ten minutes we read the stations around the cove, remove each one's known bias, and use what's left to nudge the models toward what is actually happening. Positions on the map are rounded so no single house is pinned.

How it works

Every ten minutes, in the cloud.

GatherForecasts from HRRR (hourly, fine-grained), NBM (the official blend) and the European model, plus readings from the local stations and the airports.
CorrectNudge each model toward what the stations see right now, strongest in the first five hours, then fix known habits: how errors grow with lead time, time-of-day bias, sea-breeze sun, cloud saturation.
Blend and chooseMix the corrected models with weights refit every morning from the last 14 days, or pick the one source that has been winning for that field and lead time.
GradeEvery forecast hour is logged and scored once it happens, against several truths, so a change ships only if it wins on days it was never tuned on.
What's next

Where we're working now.

Testing a homebuilt sensor. A backyard temperature, humidity and pressure sensor now feeds the network. It stays an input with a capped weight until it passes an accuracy test against independent sources.
Deciding whether the extra stations earn a place in the live forecast. They improve the network's temperature estimate. Whether that shows up as a better forecast is measured above, and we flip it on only if it does.
Humidity and dew point. Our stations and NOAA disagree on how dry the air is; a third source settles which one is right before we change anything.
